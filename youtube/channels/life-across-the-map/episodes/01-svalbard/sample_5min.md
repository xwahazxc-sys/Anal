## Script (narration)

[COLD OPEN]
In this town you can't be born, and you can't be buried. So why do more than two thousand people refuse to leave?

[TITLE — 78°N 15°E · LONGYEARBYEN, SVALBARD]
This is Longyearbyen, on the Norwegian archipelago of Svalbard. Thirteen hundred kilometres from the North Pole. Closer to the Pole than to Oslo. It is the northernmost town on Earth with a school, a hospital, a supermarket and an airport with scheduled flights. And almost everything about living here comes with a rule you have never heard of.
The town began in 1906, when an American businessman, John Munro Longyear, came here to dig coal. His name stuck. Coal is the reason the town exists, and today the mining is almost over. But the old wooden towers that once carried the coal across the valley are still standing, like a skeleton of the place it used to be.

[1 — THE DARK]
Start with the light, because for a third of the year there isn't any.
At the end of October, the sun sets here and does not come back. From mid-November to the end of January there isn't even twilight at noon. Just a deep blue, then black, for weeks. The town runs on streetlights, headlamps and the glow of windows.
Locals say the first winter is the hardest. Your body stops knowing when to sleep. Some people keep a strict routine, the same breakfast, the same walk, the same bedtime, just to remind themselves what time it is. Others leave before the second winter.
And when the sun finally returns above the horizon in February, the mountains around the valley still hide it. In town the first sunlight touches the old hospital steps around the eighth of March. People gather there to see it. They have a whole festival week for it.
So that's the first price of living here. Not the cold. The dark.

[2 — YOU CAN'T BE BORN HERE]
Now the first rule.
Longyearbyen has a small hospital. It can set a broken arm, treat a snowmobile crash, stabilise you for a flight. What it does not do is deliver babies.
So every pregnant woman in town does the same thing. A few weeks before her due date, she packs a bag and flies to the Norwegian mainland. She gives birth there, waits, and only then comes home with the baby.
Almost every child who grows up here was born more than eight hundred kilometres away.

[3 — YOU CAN'T BE BURIED HERE]
The second rule is stranger.
There is a small cemetery on the hillside above town, white wooden crosses in the snow. But since around 1950, it has not taken new burials.
The reason is the ground itself. Under Svalbard lies permafrost — earth that stays frozen all year round. Bodies buried here do not decompose the way they would anywhere else. The town found out the hard way. Among the graves are victims of the 1918 flu pandemic, and decades later scientists came to study them, because the cold had preserved far more than anyone expected.
So today, if you are seriously ill or very old, you are usually flown south to be cared for on the mainland. If someone does die here, they are flown out to be buried. People like to say dying is illegal in Longyearbyen. It isn't. There's simply nowhere to put you.

[4 — THE RIFLE LINE]
At the edge of town there are road signs with a polar bear on them. They mark a line.
Inside the line, you're in a town. Beyond it, you're in polar bear country, and the rules change. Anyone heading out is expected to carry protection: a flare gun to scare a bear away, and a rifle for when that doesn't work. Visitors rent them. Locals own them.
Even the university here, the northernmost in the world, starts every new student's year the same way: with a safety course, and a lesson on how to handle a rifle.
Polar bears are protected, and shooting one is allowed only when your life is in danger, and every single case is investigated.
So children here learn two things about the outdoors very early: never go past the signs alone, and always look behind you.
And every so often, a bear doesn't stop at the signs. It walks into the settlement itself. Word goes out, people stay indoors, and the governor's team uses a helicopter to drive the animal back out toward the ice.

[TEASE]
But the strangest rule in Longyearbyen isn't about birth, or death, or bears.
It's about who is allowed to stay.
There's no welfare office here. Almost no one retires here. If you can't support yourself, you can be refused the right to stay at all.
Because this is one of the only places on Earth where people from dozens of countries can move in and work without a visa — under a treaty signed in 1920. And yet almost no one stays for life. To understand why, you have to look at what it actually costs to survive a year at seventy-eight degrees north.

## Notes for fact-check (sources in chat, 23.10.2026)
- Population: secondary sources give 2,000–2,500 → "more than two thousand".
- Burials: cemetery stopped new burials ~1950 ("around 1950"). 1918 flu: research expedition 1998; sources disagree on whether coffins sat in permafrost → no claim that the virus was "alive".
- Polar night: sun below horizon ~late Oct → mid-Feb; no twilight ~11 Nov → 30 Jan; sun back in town ~8 March (Solfestuka at the old hospital steps).
- Births: no maternity care in Longyearbyen; women travel to the mainland weeks before due date.
- Rifle: protection required beyond settlement; flare gun first; shooting only in self-defence, investigated by the Governor.
- Svalbard Treaty 1920: nationals of treaty states have equal right to reside and work; no Svalbard visa (Schengen transit still applies).
- Founding: Longyear City 1906, Arctic Coal Company of John Munro Longyear; Norwegian coal mining ending (Mine 7 closure set for 2025) → "almost over".
- Bears entering town: occasional; Governor (Sysselmesteren) chases them out by helicopter.
- Svalbard rules allow refusing residence to people who cannot support themselves; no mainland-style welfare.
- UNIS (University Centre in Svalbard): mandatory safety course incl. rifle training for students.
- Removed the popular "more polar bears than people" line: the 2015 count on the Norwegian side was ~1,000 bears.
