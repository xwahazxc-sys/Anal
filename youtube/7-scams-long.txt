You could lose your savings to 7 scams, but only one of them almost nobody spots. Which?
I'll show you all seven, the exact messages they use, and the tell that gives each one away. The one almost nobody spots is last, and it's the one that costs the most.
Number one. The delivery text. "Your parcel could not be delivered. Update your address here." It arrives when you're actually waiting for something, which is why it works. You tap the link, and the page looks exactly like the courier. Logo, colours, tracking number. It asks for a small redelivery fee. A couple of dollars, so you don't think twice. But the fee isn't the point. The point is the card number you just typed in, and that gets used somewhere else, for a lot more than a couple of dollars. The tell is the link. Couriers don't send you to a random domain with extra words and numbers in it. If you're waiting for a parcel, open the courier's app or type their site yourself.
Number two. The bank call. Your phone shows your bank's name. A calm voice says there's a suspicious payment and they need to "verify" you. They know your name. Sometimes they know the last digits of your card. That's what makes it feel real. Here's the thing: caller ID can be faked. The name on your screen proves nothing. And the details they know usually come from old data leaks, not from your bank. Then they ask for the code your bank just texted you. That code is the key to your account. Your bank will never ask you to read it back. So here's the only sentence you need: "I'll call you back on the number on my card." A real bank says fine. A scammer argues, or hangs up. Then hang up, and call the number on the back of your card.
Number three. "Hi Mum, I dropped my phone, this is my new number." A few messages of small talk, then an urgent bill that needs paying today, and a reason they can't do it from their own account. It always comes with a reason you can't just call them. The phone is broken, the microphone doesn't work, they're in a meeting. The tell is urgency plus a new number plus money. Call the old number. If your kid answers, you're done. And agree on a family code word now, before anyone needs it, so a real emergency can be checked in five seconds.
Number four. The easy job. A friendly recruiter messages you out of nowhere and offers you money to like videos or rate hotels. You didn't apply for anything, and that's the first tell. The first payments are real. Small, but real. You're added to a group chat where other "workers" post screenshots of their earnings. Then a "task" needs you to top up your account to unlock bigger earnings. After that, there's always one more task and one more top-up. That's the switch. Any job that asks you to pay in is not a job.
Number five. The keen buyer. You list something online. A buyer agrees instantly, no haggling, doesn't even ask a question about the item. They say they'll arrange a courier and send a link to "receive your payment." The link asks for your card details, including the code on the back. You never need that to receive money. Only to send it. If a buyer won't meet in person or use the platform's own payment, walk away. A real buyer has questions. A scammer has a link.
Number six. The frozen screen. A pop-up fills your browser, sirens, a warning that your computer is infected and a number to call. Sometimes it claims to be from a company you've heard of. If you call, a helpful technician asks you to install a program so they can fix it. That program gives them control of your computer, and from there, your bank. The real software on your computer doesn't do that, and it never gives you a phone number. Close the browser. If it won't close, restart the computer. Never call the number, and never let anyone install remote access.
Number seven. The one almost nobody spots. It doesn't start with a scam. It starts with a wrong-number message. "Hi, is this Anna?" You say no. They apologise, and they're pleasant. Maybe a little funny. And because you were polite, you keep chatting. Over weeks you talk every day. They share photos of their life: dinners, travel, a nice car. They ask about your day and they remember what you told them. They're successful, they trade crypto or gold, and they never push it. It just comes up. Eventually, you ask them how it works, and they show you a platform. You put in a little. The dashboard shows profit. You can even withdraw a small amount, which is what convinces you. Then you put in more. Maybe you borrow to put in more. And when you try to take it all out, there's a "tax" or a "fee" to pay first. You pay it, and then there's another one. The platform was never real. The profit was a number on a screen. And the person you talked to every day was a script, often read by someone working through a list of numbers.
Why nobody spots it: there's no single moment that feels like a scam. Every step is small, every step is your own choice, and by the time money comes up, you trust them. So here's the tell. Someone you've never met in person, bringing up money, is the whole scam. It doesn't matter how long you've been talking. It doesn't matter how kind they've been. That kindness was the product.
So that's all seven. And every single one breaks the same rule. They want you to act inside their message: their link, their number, their platform. Each one adds pressure, too, so you act before you think. The defence is always the same. Leave the message. Slow down. Open your bank, your courier, your family's number yourself. A real problem will still be there in ten minutes. A scam won't.
And if you've already clicked, or already paid, do this in order. First, call your bank on the number on the back of your card and tell them it's fraud. Speed matters here, so do it before anything else. Second, change the password on any account you typed in, and turn on two-step verification. Third, if anyone installed software on your computer, disconnect it from the internet and get it checked. Fourth, report it. In the US that's reportfraud.ftc.gov. In the UK, forward scam texts to 7726. And last, be ready for the follow-up: people who've been scammed often get contacted by "recovery services" promising to get the money back for a fee. That's the same scam, second round. And please don't be embarrassed. These scams are built by people who do this all day. Talking about it is how the next person gets warned.
If one of these would have got someone you know, send them this video. That's the whole job.
